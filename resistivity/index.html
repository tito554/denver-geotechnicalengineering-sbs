Denver’s subsurface doesn’t read like a textbook. The transition from expansive Pierre Shale to coarse alluvial deposits along the South Platte River creates sharp resistivity contrasts that standard drilling alone can miss. We run vertical electrical sounding campaigns across the metro area—from the clay-heavy soils of Aurora to the gravelly terraces near Golden—to map these boundaries without punching a hole every ten feet. Combining resistivity profiles with targeted test pits helps us ground-truth anomalies where the geophysics flags a low-resistivity zone that could be saturated clay or buried fill, and we cross-check deep bedrock refusal with seismic refraction when the velocity model needs independent confirmation.

A VES line of three soundings across a typical Denver quarter-acre lot resolves the bedrock profile in half a day, with no cuttings and no traffic disruption.

Methodology and scope

At 5,280 feet elevation, Denver’s semi-arid climate and winter freeze-thaw cycles affect near-surface resistivity in ways that a generic curve won’t capture. Our Schlumberger arrays typically reach investigation depths of 100 to 300 feet using AB/2 spacings up to 500 feet, enough to penetrate the weathered shale contact that controls foundation bearing in much of the Denver Basin. We process each sounding with 1D inversion software constrained by borehole logs where available, then output layered resistivity models tied to USCS soil descriptions. For sites near Cherry Creek or the Platte floodplain, we integrate the VES interpretation with CPT soundings to refine the stratigraphy where sands and silts alternate rapidly, a layering that resistivity alone can smear if not calibrated against direct push data.

Local considerations

IBC Section 1803 requires that foundation investigations extend deep enough to identify compressible or expansive layers that could impair performance. In Denver, missing a lens of saturated, low-resistivity clay within a gravel terrace can mean differential heave that slab-on-grade designs didn’t anticipate. We’ve seen resistivity profiles where a 15 ohm-m layer at 12 feet—interpreted initially as moist fill—turned out to be a perched groundwater pocket that softened the subgrade over two wet seasons. Without a VES grid to map the pocket’s lateral extent, the remedial undercut would have been guesswork. ASTM D6431-18 gives us the field protocol to ensure repeatable electrode geometry and low contact resistance, even in dry surface conditions that are common from late summer through October across the Front Range.

Associated technical services

01

1D Vertical Electrical Sounding (VES) for Depth Profiling

Single-station Schlumberger soundings that resolve layer resistivity versus depth. We use these routinely to estimate depth to Denver Formation bedrock, map the top of the water table in alluvial terraces, and provide resistivity inputs for grounding grid design per IEEE 80.

02

2D Electrical Resistivity Tomography (ERT) Cross-Sections

Multi-electrode lines (up to 84 electrodes) towed or planted along a profile. Ideal for locating karst-like voids in the Lyons Formation west of town, delineating landfill boundaries, or tracking grout migration during compaction grouting beneath existing slabs.

Can resistivity distinguish between dry sand and sandstone in the Denver Basin?

Yes, but it requires calibration. Dry clean sand may read above 200 ohm-m while the weakly cemented sandstone of the Denver Formation often falls between 40 and 80 ohm-m. We constrain the interpretation with at least one borehole or outcrop observation so that the resistivity crossover isn’t misread as a grain-size change alone.

How long does a VES survey take on site?

A crew of two can complete four to six soundings in a working day, assuming reasonable access and dry surface conditions. Winter surveys with frozen ground or snow cover take longer because we have to wet or chip electrode contact points to bring contact resistance below 2,000 ohms.

Is the VES method reliable in Denver’s high-clay soils?

It’s actually one of the better methods for mapping clay because the low resistivity of saturated Pierre Shale—often below 10 ohm-m—gives a strong, unambiguous signature. The challenge is vertical resolution: thin silt seams inside the clay can be masked, which is why we pair VES with occasional direct push probes where the stratigraphy is critical.
