Denver sits on the Pierre Shale and Denver Formation—expansive claystone beds that can swell up to 10% by volume with seasonal moisture. Designing a rigid footing here is a gamble. Raft or mat foundations spread structural load across a continuous slab, reducing differential movement when the subgrade heaves. We see projects across Cherry Creek, Stapleton, and the Highlands where shallow bedrock or variable fill makes isolated footings impractical. Before committing to a mat, understanding the swelling pressure through Atterberg limits and suction testing is essential. A grain size analysis of the foundation stratum determines the fine fraction driving volume change, while correlation with Atterberg limits refines the heave prediction model so the raft thickness and reinforcement match the actual plasticity index on site.

A properly designed raft in Denver transforms the swelling clay problem from a structural threat into a predictable, manageable deformation parameter.

Methodology and scope

At 5,280 feet elevation, Denver's semi-arid climate creates a deep moisture-active zone—often extending 15 to 20 feet below grade. That depth dictates the mat's influence zone and the required modulus of subgrade reaction. A raft foundation works best here when the design accounts for edge lift and center lift modes, the two swelling patterns common in Front Range geology. In our workflow, we model the mat as a beam on elastic foundation using Winkler springs calibrated to site-specific k-values from plate load tests or SPT correlations. Key elements we specify include thickened edges to resist perimeter heave, post-tensioning to control cracking, and underslab vapor barriers paired with capillary breaks to prevent moisture migration into the slab. Where the Denver Formation dips sharply, we combine the mat with stone columns to stiffen the transition zone and reduce total settlement under dead load—critical for warehouses and mid-rise structures in the Central Platte Valley.

Local considerations

IBC Section 1805 and ASCE 7-22 Chapter 12 place Denver in Seismic Design Category C, with site class often degrading to D or E where soft clay exceeds 10 feet. The risk isn't just peak ground acceleration—it's the combined effect of seismic shaking on a partially saturated expansive soil. Cyclic loading can trigger collapse of the clay structure, leading to sudden settlement. We've investigated sites near Sloan's Lake where the groundwater table rises within 5 feet of the bottom of the mat, creating buoyancy forces that the dead load must counteract. Ignoring the hydrostatic uplift component leads to floating or cracked mats. A thorough geotechnical report must include swell-consolidation testing (ASTM D4546), suction profiles, and a liquefaction screening if loose sands are present below the clay. The mat design then incorporates a factor of safety against flotation of 1.2 or higher per ASCE 7, verified under the design flood elevation.

Why is a raft foundation better than isolated footings in Denver's soils?

Isolated footings concentrate load on small areas of expansive clay, leading to differential heave that can crack walls and slab-on-grade floors. A raft spreads the load, bridges soft spots, and provides inherent stiffness to resist both edge lift and center lift deformation modes common in the Denver Formation.

Do raft foundations need post-tensioning in Colorado?

Not always, but post-tensioning is strongly recommended when the predicted differential heave exceeds 1 inch. It introduces compressive stress that counteracts the tensile forces from soil swelling, reducing slab cracking and allowing thinner sections compared to conventionally reinforced mats.

How deep does the geotechnical investigation go for a mat foundation?

We typically drill to a depth of 20 to 30 feet below the proposed mat bottom, or until we penetrate at least 10 feet of competent bedrock. This captures the full active zone for moisture fluctuation and allows us to evaluate deep-seated settlement or heave that could affect the entire foundation block.
