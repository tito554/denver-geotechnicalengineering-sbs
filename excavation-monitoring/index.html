Denver sits at exactly 5,280 feet, but the real story is underground. The city’s western neighborhoods sit on stiff claystone and sandstone, while the eastern plains are draped in highly expansive clay and old floodplain deposits. Every deep excavation in the metro area cuts through this transition zone. We have seen 30-foot cuts near Cherry Creek move laterally within 48 hours of a summer thunderstorm. That is why our monitoring program is not a checkbox—it is an active risk management tool. Our technicians install inclinometer casings behind soldier pile walls and track deformation against IBC 2024 thresholds. When the excavation hits saturated alluvium near the Platte River, we often combine readings with a CPT test to verify the undrained shear strength profile assumed in the shoring design. If the numbers shift, the contractor knows immediately.

A tenth of an inch of movement in Denver’s expansive clay can mean 18 kips of unexpected load on a strut. We measure it every two hours.

Methodology and scope

The difference between excavating in Golden’s bedrock and Aurora’s swelling clay is night and day. In Aurora, a shoring wall designed with textbook parameters can creep inward as the clay absorbs moisture from the air itself. Our monitoring captures that slow drift before it becomes a problem. We use automated total stations and wireless tiltmeters on the steel struts. One setup near the Tech Center recorded a 0.15-inch daily deflection on a waler beam after a water main broke two blocks away. Without monitoring, the movement would have gone unnoticed until drywall cracked in the adjacent building. For cuts deeper than 20 feet in collapsible fill, we recommend coupling this service with a deep excavation design review to ensure the support system matches the observed behavior. Every data point is uploaded to a live dashboard that the superintendent can check from his phone.

Local considerations

Denver’s bedrock drops dramatically eastward, and the overlying Denver Formation contains lenses of loose sand that can flow into a cut if the shoring is not tight. In 2019, a 25-foot excavation near the Federal Center lost ground behind a sheet pile wall because a three-inch gap opened at a wale connection. The resulting subsidence damaged a gas line. Our monitoring program uses optical survey prisms on the wall face and piezometers in the retained soil to catch exactly that scenario. When pore pressure rises after a heavy rain, the data shows a spike in both movement and water level. We then alert the shoring designer to verify the drain system. The biggest risk is not the soil itself—it is assuming the soil will behave as predicted without field verification.

What triggers an automatic alert in the monitoring system?

We set three-tier thresholds: 70% of design movement triggers a notification to the project engineer, 85% triggers a call with the shoring designer, and 100% sends an immediate stop-work recommendation to the superintendent’s phone. The values are defined in the pre-construction monitoring plan.

Does Denver’s expansive clay affect the inclinometer readings?

Yes, and we account for it. Expansive clay can swell the borehole annulus and pinch the casing. We use a slightly larger borehole diameter and a stiff bentonite-cement grout mix to isolate the casing from swelling pressures. The readings are corrected for any thermal effects from the grout curing.

How long do you keep the instruments in place after excavation is complete?

We typically maintain monitoring through backfill and until the permanent structure’s floor slab is in place and lateral earth pressures are transferred. For deep excavations near sensitive structures, we may keep a few settlement points active for 90 days after completion.
