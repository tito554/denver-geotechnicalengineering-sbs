Denver sits on the Pierre Shale and Denver Formation—expansive claystone that swells 10% or more with moisture and shrinks just as dramatically in our dry climate. A lot of crews underestimate how that volume change transfers load to a tieback. We see anchors lose 30% of their design capacity within two seasons when the bond length isn't placed beyond the active clay zone. The water table along Cherry Creek and the South Platte adds hydrostatic pressure to the mix, so a design that works in Golden or Littleton can fail downtown. For deep cuts near Speer Boulevard, we combine the anchor analysis with a slope stability study to confirm the global factor of safety, especially where the claystone dips toward the excavation.

A tieback in Denver clay isn't just a steel tendon—it's a system that has to survive 10% soil swell, sulfate attack, and freeze-thaw cycles all in one design life.

Methodology and scope

Denver's freeze-thaw cycles reach down about 36 inches, so we specify a minimum unbonded length that puts the bond zone well below the frost line. In the Montclair and Hilltop neighborhoods, where old clay pits were backfilled with everything from brick rubble to fly ash, we won't sign off on an anchor design without a CPT sounding through the fill to find the competent bearing stratum. For permanent anchors, we spec double-corrosion protection per PTI DC 35.1-14, with a corrugated HDPE sheath over epoxy-coated strand, because the sulfate content in Pierre Shale groundwater attacks bare steel aggressively. A detail that makes a difference here: we stagger the anchor heads in plan by at least 4 feet, even if the theoretical stress overlap is acceptable, to avoid group effect reduction in the fissured claystone. When the site is below the Cherry Creek floodplain, we coordinate anchor installation with a dewatering and permeability plan so the drill holes don't become relief wells during construction.

Local considerations

The most expensive mistake we keep seeing on Denver jobsites is treating a passive anchor like an active one—or vice versa—just to save on the initial design fee. Passive anchors rely on movement to mobilize, and in stiff Denver clay that movement can exceed 2 inches before the anchor picks up load. If the wall facing can't take that deflection, you get cracking in the shotcrete and, worse, settlement behind the wall that drags down adjacent footings. On the 8th Avenue corridor, we've recorded lateral movements that propagated 40 feet back from the excavation face. That's a lawsuit waiting to happen. Another classic error is grouting the bond zone under low pressure in fractured sandstone of the Denver Formation; the grout bleeds into fissures, you lose confinement, and the anchor creeps under proof testing. The IBC requires lock-off loads to be verified with lift-off tests on at least 5% of anchors, and we hold that minimum even on smaller tenant-improvement excavations.

Associated technical services

01

Active Anchor Design and Testing

Design of prestressed tiebacks with lock-off loads calculated to offset active earth pressure plus surcharge. Includes sacrificial anode specification, bond length calculation in weathered Denver Formation claystone, and on-site lift-off testing to verify load transfer.

02

Passive Anchor Systems

Design of fully grouted soil nails and dowels for temporary slope stabilization in overconsolidated clay. We determine allowable deflection under working loads and verify that facing elements can accommodate the movement before the anchor engages.

03

Anchor Capacity Verification and Creep Testing

Performance and proof testing per IBC Table 1810.2.1, with 10-minute and extended 60-minute creep tests for permanent anchors in Pierre Shale. We log load-extension curves and reject anchors that show non-linear creep beyond 2 mm per log cycle of time.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between active and passive anchor design for a Denver excavation?

An active anchor is tensioned at lock-off to a specific load—typically 70–100% of the design working load—so it immediately restrains the wall. A passive anchor relies on soil deformation to generate resistance. In Denver's stiff overconsolidated clay, that deformation can be substantial before a passive anchor picks up the load. We select the type based on allowable wall deflection, proximity to existing structures, and whether the anchor is temporary or permanent. For most cut-and-cover work along Colorado Boulevard with adjacent footings, active anchors are the standard.

How much does anchor design and testing cost for a typical Denver project?

For a small excavation with 6 to 12 anchors, the design, submittal, and field testing oversight typically falls in the US$1,040 - US$3,520 range, depending on the number of test anchors and whether permanent corrosion protection is required. Larger projects with 50+ anchors and multiple wall sections scale accordingly, but the unit cost per anchor goes down because the design load cases and testing procedures are already established.

How do Denver's expansive soils affect anchor bond length?

Expansive Pierre Shale and Denver Formation claystone can lose bond strength when wetted. We place the bond zone below the active moisture variation depth—at least 12 feet deep—and design the unbonded length to pass through the zone that will experience seasonal swelling. For permanent anchors, we also pressure-grout the bond zone to at least 50 psi to improve contact with the claystone and reduce long-term creep under sustained load.

What testing is required for permanent tieback anchors in Colorado?

IBC Chapter 18 mandates performance tests on at least three anchors per soil condition, proof tests on all remaining production anchors, and extended creep tests for permanent anchors in cohesive soils. We follow PTI DC 35.1-14 for the detailed procedure: a performance test loads to 133% of the design load in increments, with creep measured at each step. For permanent anchors in Denver's claystone, we run a 60-minute creep test at the maximum test load; creep must not exceed 2 mm over the final log cycle of time. Lock-off loads are verified with a lift-off test on every anchor before the stressing equipment leaves the site.
