A five-story medical office building near the Fitzsimons campus was in framing when the structural engineer flagged a problem: the near-surface claystone under the site amplified short-period motion more than the original geophysical survey suggested. The owner had assumed a fixed-base concrete frame would meet Colorado Springs-level criteria, but Denver sits on a different beast. The basin edge effect along the Front Range can turn a design earthquake into something far more demanding for stiff vertical systems. We stepped in with a base isolation scheme that decoupled the superstructure from those ground motions, using lead-rubber bearings sized to ASCE 7-16 Chapter 17 requirements. The result was a 40% reduction in story drift without adding mass to the lateral system. For developers in the Denver metro who are done with over-engineering braces and shear walls, seismic microzonation studies provide the site-specific spectra that make isolation feasible, and when the soil profile is tricky, CPT testing pins down the shear wave velocity contrasts that feed the isolator displacement calculations. That job taught us something: Denver's seismic hazard is not about magnitude, it is about amplification, and isolation changes the math completely.

Denver's basin traps seismic energy and amplifies it. Base isolation moves the building period past the worst of that amplification, so floors stay calm when the ground does not.

Methodology and scope

Denver's growth spurt after the 1980s energy boom left downtown with towers designed to UBC-era coefficients that look optimistic by modern probabilistic maps. The city sits roughly 80 km east of the Front Range fault zone, and while the peak ground acceleration is moderate, the deep sedimentary basin under the metro area traps and elongates seismic energy. That means a 0.15g short-period spectral acceleration at rock can easily translate to 0.60g or higher at the surface for structures with periods in the 0.5 to 1.5 second range. Base isolation shifts the fundamental period of the building into the 2.5 to 3.5 second range, where spectral demands drop sharply, and the isolator displacement capacity becomes the governing criterion, not the floor accelerations. We model the upper and lower bound properties of each isolator per ASCE 7-17, run nonlinear time histories with ground motions matched to the Denver Basin amplification factors, and detail the moat wall clearances for the maximum considered earthquake. The isolator testing protocol follows the prototype and production test sequences in ASTM D4014, so every unit that goes under a Cherry Creek office or a RiNo apartment podium has documented stiffness and damping. When the site is underlain by expansive claystone, footing design for the isolation interface has to accommodate both seismic displacement and swell-shrink cycles, which we handle with a reinforced mat detailed to move without cracking the isolator plinths.

Local considerations

We have seen too many Denver projects where the structural engineer designs to the mapped Ss and S1 values without accounting for the basin amplification factors published in the USGS Denver Basin study. That shortcut produces a fixed-base design that technically passes code, but the inter-story drifts and floor accelerations end up high enough to damage non-structural systems: sprinkler lines, elevator rails, glazing. For a hospital or a data center, that is not acceptable. Base isolation is not about meeting a code minimum; it is a performance decision. The risk we flag for owners is not collapse, it is downtime. A conventionally designed building in LoDo or the Tech Center might survive a 500-year event structurally but be out of service for six months while MEP systems are repaired. An isolated building, if detailed correctly, can be operational within days. The cost delta between the two outcomes is orders of magnitude larger than the isolator line item. We push clients to think in terms of post-earthquake function, not just life safety, and the Denver basin geology makes that conversation urgent.

Frequently asked questions

What does base isolation design cost for a mid-rise building in Denver?

For a typical Denver mid-rise of 6 to 10 stories, the base isolation design package, including nonlinear time history analysis, isolator specification, and peer review documentation, runs between US$4,690 and US$8,340. The final figure depends on the number of ground motion pairs required by the peer review panel and whether we are modeling one or two isolator types for comparison. The isolator hardware itself is a separate procurement item.

How does Denver's geology affect the isolator displacement demands?

The Denver Basin's sedimentary fill, which can be over 3,000 feet thick in places, amplifies long-period energy more than a typical rock site would. This pushes up the spectral displacement demand for periods above 1 second. We account for this by using basin-specific ground motion models in the time history selection, which often results in MCE displacements 15% to 25% higher than what a generic USGS hazard curve would suggest. The moat wall clearance has to accommodate that.

Can you retrofit an existing Denver building with base isolation?

Retrofit isolation is feasible but more involved than new construction. It typically requires cutting the columns above the foundation level, installing temporary jacking systems, placing the isolators, and then detailing a new diaphragm at the isolation plane. We have done feasibility studies for unreinforced masonry buildings in Capitol Hill and older concrete frames downtown. The structural cost is significant, but for heritage structures or critical facilities where demolition is not an option, it can be the right solution.

What is the difference between ASCE 7-16 and ASCE 7-17 for base isolation in Denver?

ASCE 7-17 moved seismic isolation from Chapter 17 to a standalone Chapter 17 with refined criteria for upper/lower bound analysis and clearer guidance on moat wall impact forces. For Denver projects permitted under the 2021 IBC, the 7-17 provisions apply. The practical impact on design is that we now run more ground motion pairs and the isolator testing documentation is more prescriptive. The design philosophy did not change, but the paperwork trail got thicker.
