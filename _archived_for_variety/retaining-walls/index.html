We see it too often: a contractor pours a cantilever wall in west Denver, backfills with native claystone, and twelve months later the wall tilts forward half an inch. The problem wasn't the concrete mix or the rebar schedule. It was the soil behind it. Denver's geology is a patchwork of swelling Pierre Shale, sandy colluvium along the hogbacks, and loose alluvial fill near Cherry Creek. A retaining wall design that skips the geotechnical stage is gambling. We run test pits to log stratigraphy right where the heel will sit, and pair that with grain-size analysis to confirm whether the backfill material will drain or trap water. Structural calculations matter, but around here the soil report is what keeps a wall standing.

Denver claystone swells 4 to 8 percent by volume with seasonal moisture. A retaining wall without a drainage plan turns into a dam.

Methodology and scope

One thing you learn working with Denver soils is that free-draining backfill doesn't stay free-draining if fines migrate into it. We've cored behind old walls in Golden and found the gravel blanket completely choked with silty clay after three wet seasons. That observation changed how we specify filter fabric and weep-hole spacing. Our retaining wall design package always includes a compaction spec tied to Proctor test results for the structural fill, plus an assessment of whether the foundation soil needs a key cut into bedrock. For tall walls over 12 ft on sloping lots in Jefferson County, we bring in slope stability analysis to check global failure surfaces that a simple bearing capacity calc ignores. The wall itself is just one piece of the puzzle.

Local considerations

Capitol Hill and Washington Park sit on different planets geotechnically. Capitol Hill has the Pierre Shale close to surface: stiff, blocky, and notorious for swelling when it gets wet. Wash Park sits on sandy alluvium from the South Platte drainage, which drains well but offers lower passive resistance at the toe. A wall design that works on claystone may be underbuilt for sand. We factor in the neighborhood geology specifically: in Capitol Hill we focus on drainage and expansion control, while in Wash Park we spec a wider footing and deeper key for sliding resistance. Ignoring these differences is how you get a $15,000 wall replacement job two years after construction.

Associated technical services

01

Foundation Soil Assessment

Test pits or SPT borings at wall alignment to map bearing stratum, identify groundwater, and sample for lab testing. We log according to ASTM D2488 and classify soils for lateral pressure calculations.

02

Backfill Specification & Testing

Modified Proctor, grain-size distribution, and permeability testing on proposed backfill sources. We confirm the material meets CDOT drainage requirements before a single load arrives on site.

03

Global Stability & Drainage Check

For walls on slopes, we run limit-equilibrium models to verify that the reinforced zone doesn't sit inside a larger failure arc. Includes subdrain design recommendations tied to measured soil conductivity.

Frequently asked questions

What's the cost range for geotechnical work on a Denver retaining wall?

For a typical residential wall project, the geotechnical investigation and lab testing portion runs between US$1,030 and US$4,310, depending on wall length, height, access for drilling equipment, and how many soil units we need to characterize.

Do I still need a soils report if the wall is under 4 feet?

Denver building code exempts walls under 4 feet from a permit in many cases, but that doesn't mean the soil stops swelling. Even a 3-foot garden wall can crack if it's founded on unmoistened claystone. We recommend at least a shallow test pit and plasticity index on the foundation soil.

How do you handle expansive soils behind a retaining wall?

We specify a non-expansive structural fill zone extending at least 2 feet behind the wall, compacted in lifts with moisture control. The native expansive soil stays where it is, but we put a capillary break and filter fabric between the fill and the native material to prevent fines migration.

What's the biggest mistake you see in Denver retaining wall construction?

Backfilling without a drainage system and compacting wet clay directly against the stem. We've measured hydrostatic pressure equivalent to an extra 4 feet of soil height behind walls in spring when snowmelt saturates the backfill. That pressure was never in the original design calc.

How long does the geotechnical field work take for a wall design?

Most residential sites take one day for test pits or drilling. Lab testing runs 5 to 7 business days for standard Proctor, grain-size, and Atterberg limits. The report is typically delivered within two weeks from field work, faster if the project is permit-critical.
