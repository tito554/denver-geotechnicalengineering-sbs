A contractor in RiNo District hit a delay last fall. The subgrade looked solid—sandy gravel from the South Platte River alluvium—but the lab CBR came back at 12%. Below the 15% minimum the structural section required. The fix meant a thicker aggregate base course and four days lost re-compacting. Denver’s geology shifts block by block. You get expansive claystone in Green Valley Ranch, wind-deposited silts near Aurora, and well-graded gravels closer to downtown. A single design CBR value from a regional table won’t cut it when the soil changes at the property line. Our laboratory CBR test runs ASTM D1883 soaked and unsoaked specimens so the pavement section fits the actual material, not a textbook assumption. For projects where the subgrade sits over deeper fill, we pair the CBR with an in-situ density test to verify compaction before sampling, and use grain-size analysis to confirm the fines content isn’t skewing the swell potential.

A soaked CBR below 3% in expansive Denver claystone means the subgrade will pump fines into the base course within two freeze-thaw cycles.

Our approach and scope

The most common mistake we see on Denver metro projects is testing at a single moisture content and calling it done. ASTM D1883 requires a moisture-density curve first—usually from a modified Proctor—so the CBR specimen is compacted at the target dry density and optimum moisture. Skip that step and the CBR number is useless. Our lab runs the full sequence: compaction curve per ASTM D1557, then three-point CBR on specimens molded at optimum, slightly wet, and slightly dry of optimum. Soaked specimens sit in the water bath for 96 hours. We measure swell every 24 hours. The result is a family of CBR values plotted against density, which gives the pavement designer a real operating window, not a single fragile number. For stiff clays encountered in southeast metro subdivisions, we often recommend running Atterberg limits in parallel—the plasticity index is a strong predictor of swell during the soak phase and it explains low soaked CBR values that raw compaction data alone cannot.

Local ground factors

Denver’s east side and west side sit on completely different risk profiles. West of I-25—Lakewood, Edgewater, Wheat Ridge—you are on Denver Formation claystone with moderate to high swell potential. Soaked CBR values routinely drop below 5%. East of Tower Road toward DIA, the soils are younger Quaternary alluvium and eolian deposits; CBR can range from 8% to over 25% depending on compaction and fines content. The hazard is uniformity. A borrow source that delivered 20% CBR last month may yield 10% CBR this month if the pit hits a clay lens. Pavement designed on an optimistic CBR fails by rutting in the first wet spring. The only way to manage the risk is project-specific laboratory CBR testing on the actual material the contractor places, with both soaked and unsoaked conditions reported. The difference between those two numbers tells the designer exactly how moisture-sensitive the subgrade will be under Denver’s semi-arid climate with sudden snowmelt saturation events.

Quick answers

What does a laboratory CBR test in Denver typically cost per sample?

A standard three-point laboratory CBR test including the modified Proctor compaction curve, 96-hour soak, swell monitoring, and penetration report runs between US$140 and US$200 per sample depending on the number of specimens in the batch and turnaround time requested.

How long does the laboratory CBR test take from sample drop-off to report?

The soak period alone requires 96 hours per ASTM D1883. Including sample preparation, compaction curve determination, and data reduction, standard turnaround is five to six business days. We do offer a 48-hour expedited option for single-point CBR tests when the compaction curve is already available from prior testing on the same material.

Can you run the CBR test on material with particles larger than 3/4 inch?

ASTM D1883 applies to material passing the 19 mm sieve. If the sample contains gravel larger than 3/4 inch, we recommend a gradation analysis first to determine the oversize fraction. For material with significant plus-3/4-inch content, the in-situ field CBR test per ASTM D4429 may be more representative, or we can scalp the material and apply a correction factor per AASHTO guidance.
